Making sense of your assessment report

A short companion created by me (Dr Aana Shah) to sit alongside your full report. This explains how the report is organised and what to do with it. It is general information; your report itself is specific to you, and your follow-up is the place for personal questions.

Why the report is so detailed

An assessment looks at the whole picture. That is why we gather your history, your own account (and, where relevant, your parents' accounts too), questionnaires, and a structured interview, and weigh them together - no single part is used to decide the diagnosis on its own.

How your report is organised

  • Chief complaints, background and developmental history - how things have been from early life, along with your current concerns and goals. This matters because ADHD is "neurodevelopmental", meaning it is present from childhood, and because your history helps guide which treatment may suit you best.
  • Mental status examination - an examination that identifies any objective signs and symptoms.
  • Screening measures and cognitive tasks (the questionnaires and tasks you completed before the interview) - these support the assessment and help describe your difficulties more precisely, but they are not diagnostic on their own.
  • Structured diagnostic interview - a detailed interview that maps your experiences and helps us understand the why behind them.
  • Differential considerations and co-occurring conditions - other explanations considered alongside ADHD, such as mood and anxiety, the effects of trauma, personality factors, substance use, and so on. Difficulties with attention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity show up across many conditions, not only ADHD, so the structured interview also helps us understand whether something else, or something in addition to ADHD, might be driving them.
  • Diagnostic synthesis and diagnoses - where everything is brought together, and what was, and was not, diagnosed.
  • Recommendations - the practical plan, which may include psychoeducation, medication, psychological approaches, structure and executive-function support, adjustments at work or study, and attention to sleep and general health.
  • Follow-up and monitoring - what happens next, and how progress will be reviewed.

A few things to hold in mind

A report is a tool to help, not a verdict on who you are, and the scores and tasks within it support clinical judgement rather than replace it. If a report turns out not to be clear-cut, that does not mean nothing is going on. ADHD often sits alongside other things, such as anxiety, low mood, or sleep difficulties, and it sometimes occurs together with autism. Because some conditions can also look like one another, how you respond to treatment is itself sometimes one of the clearest ways to understand what is really happening.

Everyday matters worth raising

A few quick things and common questions worth raising with your clinician rather than working them out alone:

  • Driving, and how attention or medication might affect it
  • Alcohol, caffeine, and other substances, which can interact with symptoms and with treatment
  • Planning a pregnancy or being pregnant, if relevant, since this can affect medication choices
  • Any other medicines or health conditions you have

If medication is part of your plan

Medication is reviewed and adjusted over time, titrated up or down, or switched, depending on how you respond. Your doctor will keep an eye on practical things such as blood pressure, heart rate, sleep, and appetite. Keeping a short daily note can help, and there is a simple tracker on this site built for exactly that.

Where to find further support

  • The planning, focus, and "what this means" tools on this site, which you are welcome to use whether or not you have a diagnosis.
  • ADDitude and the Massachusetts General Hospital ADHD pages, for clear, reliable information.
  • The NHS adult ADHD pages, for straightforward overviews.
  • Pause for Perspective (Hyderabad), for local support groups.

Let your doctor know (or me), and we can help point you toward others depending on where you live.

What to do with this report

It often makes more sense the second time around, so it is worth reading more than once. As you go, jot down anything that is unclear and bring it to your follow-up, where there is time to talk it through. You are welcome to share the parts that are helpful with people you trust, and to take the recommendations at a pace that is sustainable for you, a little at a time.
